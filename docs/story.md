# FEBRUARY 30th — Story Bible

> Status: draft v1, for review. Everything here is a proposal, not a commitment.

---

## 1. Premise

**Protagonist.** You. Eleven years old. Unnamed — the game never says your name
and never says it in a title card. You're referred to as "you" and other people
call you by name a total of nine times in the whole game, and the player will
not know if those are the same name.

**Setup.** It's your birthday. **February 29th.** Your party finished a few hours
ago. The house is dark. You fell asleep in your clothes.

**Inciting incident.** At midnight you wake to knocking — three knocks, a pause,
three knocks — coming from the cupboard under your window. Not a rat. A rhythm.

**The boy.** Inside is a child, roughly your age, sitting on the floor with his
back against the wall. Your face. Your cowlick. The gap where your front right
tooth used to be. He is wearing a party outfit that is *almost* yours and
*specifically* not: hat too small, shirt inside-out, jacket hem cut uneven, socks
mismatched and both too big.

**The line.** He says: *"It's my birthday too. It's the thirtieth."*

You say: *"There's no thirtieth."*

He says: *"Not down here."*

**Threshold.** You open the door wider to see him properly. The door is nine feet
tall. The inside of the cupboard is a hallway.

## 2. The canon — why the 30th exists

The 30th is not a demon. It is not a ghost. It is **a disposal day.**

### The Overflow

A calendar is a machine with a fixed number of boxes and too many days to put in
them. Once a year there is a day the calendar cannot print. February 30th is
that day: unnumbered, uncounted, unclaimed.

Everything that does not fit into a real day is pushed there:

- birthdays that were cancelled
- parties where nobody came
- the kid who was not picked up
- the drawing nobody looked at
- the version of every person who made one different choice

**The 30th is a landfill made of almost-days.** That is the entire cosmology.
There is no ancient evil, no curse, no bloodline. It is administration. It is a
clerical error with a weather system.

### Why the protagonist is special to it

This is the load-bearing detail, and it is why his birthday is the 29th and not
a random date:

**He was born on the rarest day there is.** February 29th happens once every
four years. Leap-day children are, in a real and measurable way, the calendar's
rounding error — and it is a known, documented, human problem that **nobody
remembers their birthday.**

Grandparents forget. School forms get it wrong. There is no card in the shop.
Leap-day kids are quietly, repeatedly let down by the same mechanism that
creates the 30th.

So the 30th did not choose him. It *recognised* him. He is the day the calendar
already forgot. The Overflow is essentially a **lost-and-found bin**, and he is
an item in it.

### The boy's nature

Because a landfill needs a shape, and this landfill is made of children, it
builds one: **the Boy** is not one ghost. He is an aggregate. Every cancelled
party, every un-picked-up kid, every forgotten leap day, poured into a single
eleven-year-old shape because the Overflow could only think of one child to be.

He wears your face because you are the most recent item in the bin. He is not
your twin, your victim, or your replacement. **He is a pile of other people's
kids wearing the last kid's face.**

He is not being malicious. This is important and it must survive every rewrite.
He is being *reasonable*. He is eleven. He has been at this party alone for a
long time, and he would like for it to be somebody's birthday again.

## 3. The structure of the lie

The Boy does not chase you. **He shows you around.** That is the whole game.

The 29th is not a lair. It is a **neighbourhood assembled from every
neighbourhood where somebody's birthday went wrong.** It is a real place, built
correctly, by the calendar, out of genuine memories that all went wrong in
different ways.

The five acts are five neighbourhoods. Each one is somebody's worst birthday
night, rebuilt accurately by a system that has no concept of a bad outcome — it
only knows it must *render the day somewhere*.

The horror is not that the place is fake. **The horror is that the place is
accurate.**

## 4. The offer

By the end of Act V the Boy has explained himself completely, and he makes a
simple, reasonable request.

> *You can go home. Your real twenty-ninth is still running — it's only just
> past midnight. I will let you walk out.*
>
> *But then it's just me again.*
>
> *So somebody has to be at the party.*

He is not asking to be let out. He is asking for **a replacement guest.**

This is the moral spine of the game: the offer is not villainy, it is *scarcity*.
There is one party and no more birthdays, and he is asking you to solve a
resource problem. Refusing him is allowed. So is accepting.

### The four lifelines

Across the acts you collect four objects. Each is a reason to say no. None of
them is a weapon. The game has no combat.

| # | Object | World | What it argues |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | The Party Favour | I — 29th Street | Someone is already here. You don't have to. |
| 2 | The Rear-view Card | II — The Long Way | There are more of us than you think. |
| 3 | The Wrong Coat | III — Lost and Found | Look at what happens to the ones who wear it. |
| 4 | The Day Chart | IV — The After | You can see exactly how this ends. |

You can refuse the offer with **one** lifeline. Collecting all four changes the
final conversation, not the ending. The game respects the player's curiosity
without requiring it.

## 5. Endings

Three. Chosen at a single prompt in Act V. No other branch points in the game.

### A — "Wear the coat"

You give him your clothes and you go in the cupboard.

He walks out into your bedroom. Your mother hugs him. **She does not notice a
difference** — that is the horror, and it is the entire payoff of the face-swap
setup. He gets his birthday. It is, for about four seconds, a good party.

You are in the Overflow. You become the next knocking.

The final shot is the opening shot: a bedroom, a cupboard, knocking from inside.
The boy on the floor of the cupboard has your pyjamas and your cowlick. He says
*"it's my birthday."* The camera does not open the door.

**Cost:** you took his place voluntarily. The cycle continues, and it was always
going to.

### B — "Blow out the candles"

You blow out the candles on a cake set for a child who is not in the room.

The 30th collapses — not violently, but *administratively*, the way a bad
spreadsheet row gets deleted. You survive. You go home.

The cost is precise and horrible: **the calendar is now perfectly correct.** You
ate the 30th. There is no overflow, no spare day, no bin for things that don't
fit — and with it, your own 29th. Your mother looks at the cake and does not
know what day it is. Nobody remembers. Ever again.

**Cost:** you solved the problem by removing the safety margin. When you are in
trouble — and you will be — there will be no day left that can hold you.

### C — "Stay"

You do not trade, and you do not destroy. You take his hand.

You both walk out of the cupboard, into the real 29th, and he lives in your room
for the rest of the game. The credits play over a house with two kids in it who
are not related and cannot explain this to anyone.

The last line of the game is him asking:

> *"What day is it?"*

And you tell him the truth, because after everything he has earned the truth:

> *"The twenty-ninth."*

And he says:

> *"No it isn't."*

**Cost:** responsibility. Forever. Every 29th, at midnight, something knocks —
but it is coming from the *other* side now, and it is a different bad day, and
someone in that house knows where the cupboard is.

**This is the true ending.** It is not the "good" one. It is the *expensive* one,
and the game never tells the player which to pick.

## 6. Cast

### The Boy

Your face. Your missing tooth. Eleven. Never lies, never threatens, never
raises his voice. He is *pleased* to see you, and that is worse than if he were
hostile. He calls you by your name correctly, always, and you never learn when he
learned it.

**Performance rule:** he must never be more than three steps away from the
camera in dialogue scenes. He is not menacing. He is *near*.

### The Party Guests (Act I)

Thirty to two hundred identical adult figures, standing motionless in the street,
all facing the same direction. They are the parents from every party that ended
badly. They do not move. They do not turn.

If you enter a Guest's line of sight and stay there, the **frost** builds. Not
chase. You are simply *noticed*, and being noticed is the only danger in the
game. Leaving the cone resets it. They never pursue.

The Act I scare: when you leave, the camera cuts to a different angle. They have
moved. They are all facing the other way now.

### The Parties of the Road (Act II)

Cars that keep driving to nowhere. Every car is full of people facing forward,
windows fogged, and **one passenger too many in the back seat.** No lights inside.
They do not stop. They do not crash. They are simply always passing.

### The Waiting Children (Act III)

Twelve identical children in a school hall, all with your face, all waiting to be
collected. Exactly one belongs to this world and will help you. You must tell
them apart using evidence you carry. Getting it wrong is not death — it is a
restart of the hall, and a sound you do not want to hear again.

### The Adults of the After (Act IV)

The only world with dialogue from adults, and the only one where the player is
asked to think rather than sneak. Waiting room. Fluorescent light at the wrong
brightness. A clinic that is not a clinic.

## 7. Tone rules

Non-negotiable, for every writer and every artist on the project.

1. **The game never jumpscares without a fair tell.** Every scare has a sound cue
   or a visual tell at least 700 ms before it. The game is frightening, not cheap.
2. **The Boy is never a monster.** No attack, no chase, no gore, no death state
   in the entire game. The only "fail" states are *retries* — being noticed, being
   wrong about a child, and the three endings.
3. **The horror is accuracy, not gore.** Every disturbing image in this game is
   something real and slightly wrong: a coat with a misspelled name, a party with
   no children, a calendar printed in red. **No blood, no dismemberment, no
   realistic human faces, no jump-scare imagery.** The reference register is
   analog horror and PS1-era found footage, not body horror.
4. **The mundane is the weapon.** The scariest thing in this game is a coat on a
   rack that fits. Keep the settings domestic.
5. **The player is never told they are in a nightmare.** No "you were dreaming"
   cop-out, no twist that invalidates the previous four acts.

## 8. Open questions for review

1. **Is the Boy's friendliness right?** The alternative reading — that he *is*
   your twin and the games are wrong about him — changes Acts III and V. Current
   draft commits to the aggregate reading.
2. **Should the player be able to talk to the Guests in Act I?** Currently no.
   Isolation is scarier than dialogue, but it costs characterisation.
3. **Naming.** The protagonist is unnamed on purpose. Confirm this is the call and
   not a placeholder.
4. **Length target.** ~4–6 hours, five acts, no procedural anything. Confirm
   before any world gets built.
